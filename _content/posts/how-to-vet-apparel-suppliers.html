Short answerTo vet an apparel supplier in India: confirm they regularly make your exact product, verify their GST registration and factory address, speak to existing clients, order a paid sample and test it, agree everything in a written purchase order, and use staged payments rather than full advance. Walk away if any of these are refused.

Two factories in the same city making the same garment can deliver very different quality, timelines and communication. For a small brand, one bad order can mean returns, delays and a lost season. Vetting takes a few days. Skipping it can take months to recover from.

1. Check capability first

The most common mistake is choosing a supplier that can make clothes, but not your clothes. Ask:

  • Which categories do you produce every month? Ask for photos of current production, not a catalogue.
  • Do you knit, dye, print and stitch in-house, or outsource some steps? Outsourcing is common, but you should know who does what.
  • What is your monthly capacity, and how much is free in my timeline?
  • What is your MOQ per style and per colour?

3. Order a paid sample and test it properly

A sample is the single best predictor of bulk quality. Pay for it, and test it like a customer would:

  1. Measure every point on your size chart. Tolerances of about 1 cm are common; agree yours in advance.
  2. Feel and weigh the fabric. Ask for the GSM and check it matches.
  3. Inspect stitching: even seams, no loose threads, secure hems and necklines.
  4. Check decoration: print sharpness, colour accuracy and embroidery density.
  5. Wash it three to five times. Look for shrinkage, colour bleeding, pilling and twisting.

4. Ask the questions that reveal reliability

  • What is the lead time after sample approval, and what usually causes delays?
  • How do you handle defects or pieces that fail measurements?
  • Will bulk use the same fabric lot as the sample?
  • Who is my single point of contact, and how often will I get updates?
  • Can I see the goods, or photos and videos, before dispatch?

Clear, specific answers are a good sign. Vague answers are not.

5. Put everything in writing

WhatsApp messages are useful but not enough. A purchase order should include:

  • Style, fabric, GSM, colours and decoration, referencing the approved sample.
  • Quantity and size split.
  • Unit price, taxes and what is included (labels, packing, freight).
  • Delivery date and the agreed quality standard.
  • Payment terms and what happens with defects.

6. Use safer payment terms

With a new supplier, avoid paying 100% upfront. A deposit to start production and the balance after you approve finished goods is common and protects both sides. For larger orders, an inspection before the final payment is worth the cost.

Red flags: when to walk away

  • Refuses to make a sample, or wants a large order before showing quality.
  • Quotes change after you agree, or extras appear late.
  • No verifiable GST details or factory address.
  • Pushes for full advance payment and urgency.
  • Cannot name current clients or show current production.
  • Avoids written purchase orders.
  • Sample quality is far above what bulk pricing could realistically support.
