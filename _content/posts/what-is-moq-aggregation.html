Short answerMOQ aggregation, also called order pooling, means combining orders from several buyers so that together they meet a factory's minimum order quantity (MOQ). The factory runs one efficient production order; each buyer receives only the pieces they committed to, at a price close to bulk.

Minimum order quantities are one of the biggest barriers for small fashion brands in India. A brand that can sell 100 hoodies a season cannot place a 500-piece order without tying up cash in stock it does not need. MOQ aggregation solves the mismatch from the other side: instead of asking the factory to lower its minimum, it brings the factory more buyers.

How MOQ aggregation works

In apparel, a pooled order usually runs in five stages:

  1. A base product is defined. For example, a 240 GSM oversized cotton t-shirt made by a knitwear unit in Tiruppur, with a 500-piece minimum.
  2. Buyers commit quantities. Each brand adds the number of pieces it wants, along with its size split and any options the lot allows.
  3. The lot fills. Commitments add up until the combined quantity reaches the factory MOQ.
  4. Production runs as one order. Fabric is bought, cut and stitched as a single run, which is what keeps unit costs low.
  5. Each buyer receives their share. Pieces are sorted, packed and dispatched per brand.

What is shared and what stays yours

Aggregation only works if the parts of a garment that drive setup cost are shared. Everything else can be individual.

Because customers see your print, your label and your branding, two brands in the same lot can still look completely different on the shelf.

Why factories like pooled orders

Factories set MOQs because a production run has fixed costs: line setup, fabric minimums, dye batches and screens. A pooled lot meets that minimum, so the run is just as efficient as one from a large buyer. For the factory, aggregation means:

  • Filled capacity during slower months.
  • Orders that meet their MOQ without negotiating with dozens of small buyers.
  • Access to a pipeline of growing brands that may become direct customers later.

Risks and how to manage them

  • Fill time. A lot for a niche product may take longer to fill. Popular basics fill faster. Plan launches with this buffer.
  • Less design freedom. You work within the shared base. If you need a unique fabric or pattern, a direct factory order may suit you better.
  • Quality consistency. Because one factory makes the whole lot, quality depends on that factory. Look for a coordinator who checks quality before dispatch.
  • Clear terms. Understand how long the lot stays open, when you pay, and what happens if specifications change.

When MOQ aggregation is the right choice

Pooling is a strong fit when you need fewer than about 300 pieces per style, you are selling proven product types like tees, hoodies, joggers or shirts, and you care about factory quality and margin. It is less suited to one-off couture pieces or garments built on a fabric no one else is using.
